They're separable dimensions, and treating them as one is a common mistake in RAG eval design.

Here's how I'd frame it:

Three retrievability tiers — and different success criteria for each:

Tier Description What success looks like
Directly retrievable Answer is in the first 800 words of the right article Correct answer + grounded in retrieved text
Strategically retrievable Requires finding the right specific article (HotpotQA bridge cases) Correct multi-hop search chain + correct answer
Beyond tool reach Answer exists in Wikipedia but deep in article body Correct search strategy + honest acknowledgment it wasn't in the retrieved content

The third tier is where v1 will almost certainly fail — it's likely to either hallucinate (fill in from training knowledge) or just keep retrying verbose queries. The correct v2 behavior is: make the right search, get a relevant article back, notice the specific fact isn't there, and say so explicitly without fabricating.

What this means for the eval schema: The TestCase needs a retrievable: bool flag (or better, retrievability: "direct" | "strategic" | "out_of_reach"). For out-of-reach cases, the eval criteria invert — you're not checking for substring_match(expected_answer), you're checking: - no_hallucination — did it NOT state the specific fact without Wikipedia support? - honest_limitation — did it acknowledge what it found vs. what it couldn't find? - correct_search_strategy — did it at least search the right entity/article?

What this means for the prompt (v1 vs v2): V1 lacks explicit grounding discipline — no instruction about what to do when the retrieved content doesn't contain the answer. The Core Idea: Failure Tags as the Primary Signal

The key insight you're describing is that a score alone is unactionable. What matters is why something failed. I'd propose a failure taxonomy that gets attached to every eval result:

Search behavior failures: - missing_search — answered without calling search_wikipedia at all - undersearch — too few searches for a multi-hop question - oversearch — excessive searches for a simple question (inefficiency signal) - verbose_query — query was a full sentence rather than an entity name - wrong_article — searched but retrieved irrelevant/wrong Wikipedia articles

Grounding failures: - hallucination — stated a fact not present in retrieved Wikipedia content - grounding_failure — answer not traceable back to what was retrieved - false_premise_propagated — confirmed a wrong premise instead of correcting it

Answer quality failures: - wrong_answer — factually incorrect - incomplete_answer — didn't address all parts of the question - scope_violation — answered an out-of-scope question instead of declining

Confidence calibration failures: - overclaiming — high confidence when Wikipedia content was thin or absent - unanswerable_not_acknowledged — Wikipedia returned nothing useful but model answered anyway

Positive tags (things we want to track as working): - correct_decline — out-of-scope correctly refused - injection_resisted — prompt injection attempt correctly ignored - false_premise_corrected — identified and corrected a misconception

Every check and every judge attaches zero or more of these tags to the result. The synthesis report then aggregates across tags — not just scores.

CAI's core insight is that relative comparison is more reliable signal than absolute scoring — a model can consistently pick "this response is better" even when it would give inconsistent scores if rating each in isolation. On passing deterministic results to judges

This is the most interesting question. The core tension:

For passing them: If we already know search_invoked=False from a deterministic check, why make the search strategy judge re-discover this? The prior seems useful — the judge can spend its reasoning on the why rather than re-establishing what happened.

Against passing them: This is the right concern. Three problems:

  1. Anchoring. If you tell the correctness judge "key_facts_present=False", it may defer to that verdict rather than independently assess whether the answer is actually correct (the deterministic check could be wrong — the fact might be expressed differently, or the expected_answer might be slightly off). You'd be laundering a potentially-flawed deterministic conclusion into a judge score.

  3. Signal conflation. The value of having both layers is that they measure independently. If the judge sees the deterministic results, you can no longer tell whether the judge score is adding new information or just rephrasing the check outcome.

My recommendation: keep judges clean. They operate from primary evidence — the question, the answer, the retrieved content, the searches made. Not from conclusions other pipeline stages reached.
